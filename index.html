The 4% rule has a premium problem

Early retirement spreadsheets price food, housing, and a vacation line, then treat health coverage as somebody else's problem until 65. At 54, a mid-tier marketplace plan runs about $1,400 a month in many counties, which is $16,800 a year before a single copay. At a 4% withdrawal rate, funding that one line takes $420,000 of portfolio. The premium is a portfolio line item, and it behaves worse than the grocery line because in most years it grows faster than inflation.

Retiring at 50 means buying roughly 15 years of coverage before Medicare at 65: $16,800 times 15 is $252,000 of premiums, and that figure assumes the price stands still, which it has never done. The bridge income math after a late-career layoff has the same shape. The gap between the last paycheck and Medicare gets funded out of savings at the worst possible moment, right before the sequence-of-returns decade that decides whether the whole plan holds.

The subsidy math adds a second twist. Premium tax credits phase with income, so extra capital gains in a bridge year can shrink the credit at a bad exchange rate. Keeping withdrawals low and taxable income boring through the bridge years is worth more than most yield-chasing. Every $100 a month trimmed from the premium line cuts $1,200 a year of spending, and at 4% that is $30,000 the portfolio never has to carry.

Food spending can drop in a bad market year. The policy renews at full price. The withdrawal-strategy guides on FireNomics price the 4% rule against 50-year retirements and sequence risk, and the premium line is the expense that ignores the plan's good years entirely.
